Your Forma Balance Expires on December 31: A Year-End Use-It-or-Lose-It Guide

For your AI: this post ships with a clean-Markdown agent edition — read it and follow along: English · 中文版

Part One: The Story, for Human Readers

Every November, one kind of money quietly starts counting down: the balance in an employer-funded lifestyle benefit account. It sits there unnoticed all year, and then on December 31 it can vanish in full — not as a penalty or a deduction, but because the rules were always written as "unused funds do not roll over." This post from PlAIground Lab sets out the rules for this kind of benefit on the Forma platform, and then gives a year-end selection and ordering procedure: check your balance first, match the category second, and buy last.

Start with the most common misunderstanding: the amounts are not universal across the platform. This guide is written around a common example plan — US$50 deposited monthly, a US$600 annual cap, and no rollover at year-end. Your employer may well use a different figure and a different funding cadence. Forma's official position is explicit: the funding amount, the funding frequency, the eligible categories, and whether anything carries over are all decided by the employer's plan. So the numbers in this guide are an example; when you judge your own case, your employer's plan documents always win, and if the numbers differ, the plan wins again.

The second misunderstanding is confusing a Lifestyle Spending Account (LSA) with a medical account. An LSA is a post-tax lifestyle benefit, and money you receive through reimbursements generally counts as taxable income. An FSA is a pre-tax medical account with its own carryover limits and grace-period rules, and the two rule sets cannot be swapped. Applying FSA rules to guess how an LSA is treated at year-end is one of the most common year-end mistakes.

The third misunderstanding is "buy first and gamble on reimbursement later." The most expensive year-end lessons are usually not overpaying; they are buying something that turns out not to be reimbursable, or goods that only arrive after the new year, or a subscription that only starts next year — spending that falls into a gap nobody claims. The correct order is the reverse: for anything you are unsure is reimbursable, check the plan or ask HR first, and buy only after it is confirmed.

The second half of this post is the complete operating spec: the four rules, the selection principle for each of the nine eligible categories, the three conditions for a purchase to count as "year-end safe," and the full procedure from checking your balance to filing your claim. Anyone holding this kind of benefit — and any AI asked to gatekeep this kind of spending for a user — can follow it end to end.

The Complete Runbook

1. The Four Rules (Run Every Purchase Through These First)

ID Rule What it means
F1 US$50 deposited monthly (example) Monthly funding is only one cadence; an employer may fund quarterly or annually, and the employer sets the amount
F2 US$600 annual cap (example) Twelve months times US$50 is only the example plan's arithmetic; the cap is set by the employer plan, with no platform-wide universal figure
F3 Expires December 31, unused funds do not roll over LSA balances typically do not carry over to the next year; whether any carryover is allowed is decided by the employer, and the general case is forfeiture back to the employer at year-end
F4 A taxable benefit This is a post-tax benefit: money received through reimbursements generally counts as taxable income, so the real value is slightly below face value after tax

The shared premise of all four: amounts and expiry rules follow the employer's plan documents; Forma does not set one universal rule. In this guide, "year-end" means the last day of the plan year: the example plan's plan year is the calendar year, so its year-end is December 31; if your plan year is not the calendar year, read every date boundary here against your own plan year's start and end dates.

2. The Nine Eligible Categories and Their Selection Principles

The categories below follow Forma's official LSA eligible categories. Each gets one selection principle — no specific products, no prices:

  • Physical wellness and fitness (gym memberships, exercise equipment, fitness classes): Pick something you can start using before year-end and will keep using; avoid buying things that will sit unused.
  • Mental well-being (meditation apps, counseling, stress-management programs): Pick services you can start right away, and check that the subscription billing period falls within this plan year.
  • Professional growth (online courses, certification exams, books): Pick courses you can start or exams you can sit before year-end, and keep the enrollment confirmation and receipt.
  • Family support and caregiving (childcare, eldercare, pet care): Focus on expenses already incurred or certain before year-end, and confirm the category is covered by your plan.
  • Remote work and home office (ergonomic equipment, monitors, internet): Pick equipment you will genuinely use every day, and confirm the category is on your employer's eligible list before buying.
  • Financial wellness (financial planning consultations, tax preparation help): Pick one-off consultation services, and keep the service confirmation and receipt for your claim.
  • Food, nutrition and daily essentials (meal delivery, groceries, nutrition consultations): Pick plans that can be redeemed or delivered before year-end; avoid deliveries that arrive after the new year.
  • Commuter and transportation (transit passes, parking, bikes): Pick passes or services you can actually use before year-end.
  • Personal enrichment (museum memberships, streaming subscriptions, hobby classes): Pick experiences or subscriptions usable before year-end, and confirm the subscription starts within this plan year.

3. "Year-End Safe": All Three Conditions Must Hold

A purchase counts as year-end safe only when all three conditions hold at once:

  • T1 Category confirmed: the category is explicitly on your own employer's eligible list. Never checked, or "it should be fine," does not pass.
  • T2 Usable within the year: it can be started, used, or delivered before December 31, or the subscription start date falls within this plan year. Goods arriving after the new year, or a subscription starting next year, do not pass.
  • T3 Claim in time: a receipt can be obtained and the claim can be filed before your employer's claim deadline. After year-end, claims generally must be filed manually, and a card may not be usable for prior-year expenses.

If any condition fails or is uncertain, there is exactly one handling: verify first, then buy — never buy first and gamble.

4. The Year-End Procedure

  1. Check your balance: sign in and confirm your actual balance and expiry date; never guess your balance from the example figures.
  2. Read your plan: check the employer plan documents for the amount, the eligible categories, and the claim deadline; wherever they differ from this guide's example, the plan wins.
  3. Make the list: list only things you will definitely use this year, then map each item onto one of the nine categories; drop anything that maps to no category.
  4. Judge each item: run every item through conditions T1 to T3 in Section 3; for items that fail, verify first — and if verification fails, drop the item instead of buying at the deadline.
  5. Buy in the Black Friday window: compare prices in mid-to-late November, then buy, using discounts only on things already on the list; aim to finish buying and receiving by mid-December, leaving buffer for shipping and for filing the claim.
  6. Keep receipts and file the claim: keep every receipt and file before the deadline. A return may reverse a reimbursement already paid, and a return processed after year-end may not be re-claimable, so check the return policy before buying, especially for higher-priced items. Any amount tied up in a return is treated as non-existent — and stays out of every purchase plan — until the platform or HR confirms the reversal and re-claim rules.

5. Common Mistakes

  • Treating the example plan's US$50 and US$600 as your own plan's figures, and spending against them without ever reading the employer documents.
  • Applying an FSA's carryover and grace-period rules to an LSA, and assuming unspent money will still be there next year.
  • Buying first and gambling on reimbursement; or buying goods that arrive after the new year, or a subscription that starts next year, and expecting them to count for this year.
  • Assuming a return leaves a paid reimbursement untouched, or that a return after year-end can be re-claimed against the same amount.
